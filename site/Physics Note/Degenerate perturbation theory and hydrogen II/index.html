    Degenerate perturbation theory and hydrogen II

    Meaning of spin-orbit term

    Goal: To derive spin-orbit term (perturbed energy)

    Starting from the total angular momentum, we have $\(J^2 = L^2 + S^2 +2 \vec{L}\cdot \vec{S}\)$$\(\implies \boldsymbol{L} \cdot \boldsymbol{S} = \frac{1}{2} \left( J^2 - L^2 - S^2 \right)\)$ Since they are equal, the eigenvalue of \(\boldsymbol{L} \cdot \boldsymbol{S}\) and \(\frac{1}{2} \left( J^2 - L^2 - S^2 \right)\) are actually the same, i.e. the eigenvalue is$$ \frac{\hbar^{2}}{2} \left[ j(j+1) - l(l+1) - s(s+1) \right] = \frac{\hbar^{2}}{2} \left[ j(j+1) - l(l+1) - \frac{3}{4} \right] $$ Recall from Degenerate perturbation theory and hydrogen that \(H'_{\text{so}} = \frac{e^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0 c^2 m^2 r^3} \boldsymbol{S} \cdot \boldsymbol{L}\) and from Time-independent perturbation theory that \(E_{nlj,\mathrm{so}}^{1} = \langle \psi_{nlj} | H_{\mathrm{so}}' | \psi_{nlj} \rangle\), we can combine them as $$ E_{nlj,\mathrm{so}}^{1} = \langle \psi_{nlj} | H_{\mathrm{so}}' | \psi_{nlj} \rangle = \frac{e^{2}}{8\pi\epsilon_{0} c^{2} m^{2}} \frac{\hbar^{2}}{2} \left[ j(j+1) - l(l+1) - \frac{3}{4} \right] \langle \psi_{nlj} | r^{-3} | \psi_{nlj} \rangle $$ We then evaluate the bra-ket on the RHS. Recall from Finding the Hydrogen Wavefunction that the expectation value of r, \(\langle r \rangle\) is \(\langle \psi_0^* | r | \psi_0 \rangle\), the above equation gives $$ E_{nlj,\text{so}}^{1} = \frac{e^2 \hbar^2}{16 \pi \epsilon_0 c^2 m^2} \frac{j(j+1) - l(l+1) - 3/4}{l(l+1/2)(l+1) n^3 a^3} $$ Using the definition of Bohr radius, \(a\), \(e^2/4 \pi \epsilon_0 = \hbar^2/a m\) and \(E_n^0 = -\hbar^2/2 m a^2 n^2\) (which is obtained from the unperturbed Hamiltonian in Time-independent perturbation theory where \(\hat{H} = -(\hbar^2/2\mu) \nabla^2 - e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0 r\) and \(H\psi = E\psi\)), the final equation is $$ E_{nlj,\text{so}}^1 = \frac{(E_n^0)^2 n \left[ j(j+1) - l(l+1) - \frac{3}{4} \right]}{mc^2 \, l(l + \frac{1}{2})(l+1)} $$ and this is the spin-orbit term.

    Summarising these messy shits

    So bascially we have unperturbed Hamiltonian and energy as defined as $\(H^{0} \psi_{n}^{0} = E_{n}^{0} \psi_{n}^{0}\)$ and we have a perturbed energy as defined by $\(E_{nlj,\mathrm{so}}^{1} = \langle \psi_{nlj} | H_{\mathrm{so}}' | \psi_{nlj} \rangle\)$ The goal is to derive the perturbed energy in term of unperturbed one. To start, we first realise that Hamiltonian of perturbed energy is (obtained from magnetic potential energy) \(H'_{\text{so}} = -\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_s \cdot \underline{\boldsymbol{B}}\), where \(\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_s = -\frac{e}{m_e} \boldsymbol{S}\) and \(L = r m_e v = \frac{r m_e 2\pi r}{P} = \frac{2\pi m_e r^2}{P} = \frac{2\pi m_e r^2 I}{e} = \frac{4\pi m_e r^3 B}{\mu_0 e}\). By combining those, we have $\(H_{\text{so}}' = \frac{e^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0 c^2 m^2 r^3} \boldsymbol{S} \cdot \boldsymbol{L}\)$ What else to do? We find \(H'_{SO} \psi_{nlm} = E'_{SO} \psi_{nlm}\) by finding the eigenvalue (since \(E'\) is the eigenvalue). We do this by starting from total angular momentum \(J^2\). The eigenvalue of \(\mathbf{S} \cdot \mathbf{L}\) is then like this $\(\hat{\mathbf{S} \cdot \mathbf{L}} = \frac{\hbar^{2}}{2} \left[ j(j+1) - l(l+1) - \frac{3}{4} \right]\)$ Subs into \(H_{\text{so}}'\) and by realising the definition of perturbed energy, we do the bra-ket operation, subs in Bohr radius, find initial Hamiltonian from the defined unperturbed Hamiltonian to get the final result $$ E_{nlj,\text{so}}^1 = \frac{(E_n^0)^2 n \left[ j(j+1) - l(l+1) - \frac{3}{4} \right]}{mc^2 \, l(l + \frac{1}{2})(l+1)} $$

    Correction to the electron-proton potential energy (Darwin's term)

    Very well, congrats of getting here after those messy fucking operations. I promise what you learn so far will make you easily understand this part. No surprise that other than the SOC, there is another correction (perturbation) to be considered: Darwin term defined as $$ H_D^1 = \frac{\pi \hbar^2}{2 m^2 c^2} \frac{e^2}{4 \pi \epsilon_0} \delta(\boldsymbol{r}) $$ and this only happens when the electron is at the same position as the proton

    Similarly, recall that \(E_{nlj,\mathrm{so}}^{1} = \langle \psi_{nlj} | H_{\mathrm{so}}' | \psi_{nlj} \rangle\), we get $$ E_n^1 = \frac{\pi \hbar^2}{2m^2 c^2} \frac{e^2}{4\pi \epsilon_0} \int_V \psi_{nlm}^* \, \delta(r) \, \psi_{nlm} \, dV $$ For the integral part, take a look at the behavior. Since we have said that it happens when the electron is at the same position as the proton, it only acts at the origin. So based on the Laguerre solution \(R_{nl}\) in Finding the Hydrogen Wavefunction, we look for the value of \(l\) when \(R_{nl}(r = 0) \neq 0\), which obviously only \(l = 0\) work. So $$ E_n^1 = \frac{\pi \hbar^2}{2 m^2 c^2} \frac{e^2}{4 \pi \epsilon_0} |\psi_{n00}(0)|^2 $$ The significance is like this: there is a probability of electron appearing in the nucleus in a region very close to the nucleus and the probability density is spread out (zitterbewegung effect).

    At \(r=0\), all the Laguerre polynomials for \(l=0\) are \(L_{n-1}^1(0)\) and this is the constant term in the polynomial. Hence we get \(|\psi_{n00}(0)|^2 = 1/\pi a^3 n^3\) and $$ E_n^1 = \frac{\pi \hbar^2 e^2}{2 m^2 c^2 4 \pi \epsilon_0} \cdot \frac{1}{\pi a_0^3 n^3} = \frac{\hbar^4}{2 m^3 c^2 a_0^4 n^3} $$ Recall that \((E_n^0)^2 = \hbar^4/(4m^2 a^4 n^4)\), hence $\(E_n^1 = \frac{c}{mc^2}(E_n^0)^2n\)$

    Relativistic corrections to kinetic energy

    Right, the end of this chapter (finally). We look at relativistic corrections to kinetic energy \(E^2 = (T + mc^2)^2 = p^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4\): $$ T + mc^2 = mc^2 \sqrt{1 + \frac{p^2}{m^2 c^2}} $$

    We expand it (using binomial expansion or taylor series): $\(T \approx \frac{p^2}{2m} - \frac{1}{8}\frac{p^4}{m^3 c^2} = T^0 - \frac{1}{2m}(T^0)^2\)$ Here we can see that the second term \(H'_r = (T^0)^2/2m\) is the lowest order correction term. We make a little operation here: \(H'_r = (H^0 - V(r))^2/2mc^2\). Ahhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhh everything is easily here: \(E_{nlj,\mathrm{so}}^{1} = \langle \psi_{nlj} | H_{\mathrm{so}}' | \psi_{nlj} \rangle\) do this shit but in integral form. If we do it correctly (dont think too much, just expand the term and then separate the integral, since you have three terms after expanding the square term), you will get $$ E_{\text{r}}^1 = -\frac{1}{2mc^2} \left[ (E_n^0)^2 - 2E_n^0 \langle V(r) \rangle + \langle V(r)^2 \rangle \right] $$

    Here: - \(\langle V(r) \rangle = [-e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)]\langle 1/r\rangle\) and \(\langle 1/r\rangle = 1/an^2\) based on virial theorem - \(\langle V(r)^2 \rangle = [-e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)]^2\langle 1/r^2\rangle\) and \(\langle 1/r^2\rangle = 1/[a^2 n^3 (l +1/2)]\)

    Subs these, \(a\) and \(E^0\) into the corrected kinetic energy, you get $$ E^1 = -\frac{(E_n^0)^2}{2mc^2} \left[ \frac{4n}{l+1/2} - 3 \right] $$

    In summary

    The perturbations of hydrogen atoms are: - Spin-orbit coupling - Darwin term - Relativistic kinetic energy

    And their perturbed energy are: - $$ E_{nlj,\text{so}}^1 = \frac{(E_n^0)^2 n \left[ j(j+1) - l(l+1) - \frac{3}{4} \right]}{mc^2 \, l(l + \frac{1}{2})(l+1)} $$ - $\(E_n^1 = \frac{c}{mc^2}(E_n^0)^2n\)$ - $$ E^1 = -\frac{(E_n^0)^2}{2mc^2} \left[ \frac{4n}{l+1/2} - 3 \right] $$
