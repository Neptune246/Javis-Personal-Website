    Transition Rate Einstein Coefficient

    Stimulated Emission

    So far we have been using $$ P_{b \to a}(\omega, t) = \frac{|p_z|^2 E_0^2}{\hbar^2} \frac{\sin^2[(\omega - \omega_0)t/2]}{(\omega - \omega_0)^2} $$ where \(E_0\) has no energy form. Here we make use of energy density of electromagnetic waves in classical electrodynamics. Here we only consider the electric field $$ \frac{U}{V} = \frac{\epsilon_0 E_0^2}{2} \implies E_0^2 = \frac{2U}{\epsilon_0 V} $$ The energy density of blackbody radiation is also given by Planck's radiation $$ \frac{U}{V} = \rho(\omega) = \frac{\omega^2}{\pi^2 c^3} \frac{\hbar\omega}{\exp(\hbar\omega/k_B T) - 1} $$ By expressing \(E_0^2\) in terms of Planck's radiation, we get $$ P_{b \to a}(\omega, t) = \frac{2|p_z|^2}{\epsilon_0 \hbar^2} \rho(\omega) \frac{\sin^2[(\omega - \omega_0)t/2]}{(\omega - \omega_0)^2} $$

    [!note] To account for the full spectrum of the blackbody radiation, we have to integrate over all photon frequencies $$ P_{b \to a}(t) = \frac{2|p_z|^2}{\epsilon_0 \hbar^2} \int_0^\infty d\omega \, \rho(\omega) \frac{\sin^2[(\omega - \omega_0)t/2]}{(\omega - \omega_0)^2} $$

    Anyways, let's return to $$ P_{b \to a}(\omega, t) = \frac{2|p_z|^2}{\epsilon_0 \hbar^2} \rho(\omega) \frac{\sin^2[(\omega - \omega_0)t/2]}{(\omega - \omega_0)^2} $$ this equation, the \(\sin^2\) term, in long-time limit can be approximated as $$ \frac{\sin^2[(\omega - \omega_0)t/2]}{(\omega - \omega_0)^2} \approx \frac{\pi t}{2} \delta(\omega - \omega_0) $$ $$ \implies P_{b \to a}(t) = \frac{2|p_z|^2}{\epsilon_0 \hbar^2} \rho(\omega_0) \frac{\pi t}{2} $$ To account for 3D blackbody radiation photons $$ \langle |p_x|^2 \rangle = \langle |p_y|^2 \rangle = \langle |p_z|^2 \rangle = \frac{1}{3} \langle |p|^2 \rangle $$ and by taking the derivative to find the transition rate

    [!note] $$ R_{b \to a} = \frac{dP_{b\rightarrow a}}{dt} = \frac{\pi |p_{ba}|^2}{3 \epsilon_0 \hbar^2} \rho(\omega_0) = B_{ab} \rho(\omega_0) $$

    Absorption

    Rate of absorption is the same as spontaneous emission as \(|p_{ab}|^2 = |p_{ba}|^2\) so $$ R_{a \to b} = \frac{dP_{a\rightarrow b}}{dt} = \frac{\pi |p_{ab}|^2}{3 \epsilon_0 \hbar^2} \rho(\omega_0) = B_{ba} \rho(\omega_0) $$

    Spontaneous Emission

    Everything is simple now. At equilibrium condition, we have$$ N_b A + N_b B \rho(\omega_0) = N_a B \rho(\omega_0) \implies N_b A = B \rho(\omega_0)(N_a - N_b) $$ where - \(N_b A\) atoms leave state \(\psi_b\) by spontaneous emission.

    • \(N_b B \rho(\omega_0)\) atoms leave state \(\psi_b\) by stimulated emission.

    • \(N_a B \rho(\omega_0)\) atoms enter state \(\psi_b\) by absorption of blackbody radiation.

    For the second and third situations, we include \(\rho(\omega_0)\) because the atoms in state \(\psi_b\) are stimulated to emit or absorb photons, the rate of these processes is proportional to the availability of photons at that specific energy i.e. how many photons from that electromagnetic waves have that specific energy.

    Now we rearrange the equation to make \(\rho(\omega_0)\) the subject $$ \Rightarrow \quad \rho(\omega_0) = \frac{A}{B} \frac{1}{(N_a/N_b) - 1} $$ from statistical physics $$ \frac{N_a}{N_b} = \frac{\exp(-E_a/k_B T)}{\exp(-E_b/k_B T)} = \exp\left(\frac{E_b - E_a}{k_B T}\right) = e^{\hbar \omega_0 / k_B T} $$ we have $$ \rho(\omega_0) = \frac{A}{B} \frac{1}{e^{\hbar \omega_0 / k_B T} - 1}, \quad B = \frac{\pi |p|^2}{3 \epsilon_0 \hbar^2} $$ by equating this planck radiation formula, we have $$ A = \frac{\hbar \omega_0^3 B}{\pi^2 c^3} = \frac{\omega_0^3 |p|^2}{3 \pi \epsilon_0 \hbar c^3} $$ where the lifetime is \(\tau = 1/A\)
