    Imaging Systems

    Abel realised the important of having wide aperture to improve the diffraction limit of a lens i.e. the resolution of the lens $\(\Delta x = \frac{\lambda}{2NA}\)$ where \(NA = n \sin \Lambda\) is the numerical aperture, \(n\) is the refractive index and \(\Lambda\) is the fringe separation.

    The physical reasoning behind this is that the lights from \(k_1\) and \(k_2\) paths interfere each other to form \(\cos\) fringes. In order to increase the resolution, we need small fringes i.e. \(\Lambda \downarrow \implies \alpha \uparrow \implies k_1 \ \text{and} \ k_2 \ \text{larger} \implies D \uparrow\)

    Due to this finite size of aperture, our image is not 100% sharp. Hence instead of \(f(x',y')\) which we learnt in Intensity and Angular Spectrum, we need to include an additional transmission term after the lens \(t(x',y')\): $\(\begin{aligned} f(x', y') &= f_i(x', y') \cdot t(x', y') \ E^{(f)} \\ \implies&\propto \mathcal{F}[f_i(x', y')] * psf(u, v) \end{aligned}\)$ where \(psf(u,v) = \mathcal{F}[t(x',y')]\) is the point spread function of the lens. Therefore the goal is to make the point spread function as small (close to Dirac-delta) as possible to make the image to be what it supposed to be.

    Since normal people will use a circular lens with diameter \(D\), we model our transmission \(t(x',y')\) function as a circ function and hence the point spread function is $\(psf = \frac{\pi D^2}{4} \text{jinc} \left( \frac{\pi D \rho}{\lambda f} \right)\)$ Why don't we de-convolve the \(psf\) to get the real image? It is hard, that's it.

    Angular Resolution

    [!Definition] Resolving power of a circular instrument

    For two distant objects, we approximate the the lights travelling from there to our lens are plane waves. As they are focussed to the Fourier plane, they are a dirac-delta function shifted by some distances from the centre.

    As the \(psf\) is a jinc function, the result is a convolution of the \(psf\) and and delta function, which produces Airy disk and the images are said to be just 'resolved' when the second image is at the first minima of the first image i.e. $\(\theta_R = 1.22 \frac{\lambda}{D}\)$ This is the Rayleigh criterion.
