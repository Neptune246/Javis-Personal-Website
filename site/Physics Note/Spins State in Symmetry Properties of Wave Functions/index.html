    Spins State in Symmetry Properties of Wave Functions

    Now we include spins in the symmetry properties in wave functions as discussed above. Recall that the wave function for bosons is $\(\psi(r, r') = \frac{\phi_1(r)\phi_2(r')+\phi_1(r')\phi_2(r)}{\sqrt{2}}\)$

    and for fermions $\(\psi(r, r') = \frac{\phi_1(r)\phi_2(r')-\phi_1(r')\phi_2(r)}{\sqrt{2}}\)$

    Now we want to include the spin state for each of the particles: $\(\psi(\vec{r})\alpha \quad , \quad\psi(\vec{r})\beta\)$ where \(\alpha, \beta\) are spin-up(-down) vectors. For two electrons in an atom or molecule, they experience the attractive coulomb potential from the nucleus:

    \[ v(\vec{r}) = -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \sum_i \frac{Z_i}{|\vec{r} - \vec{R}_i|} $$ This is the general form of coulomb potential which accounts for molecules or solids where multiple nuclei are present. The Hamiltonian for each individual particle becomes $$ \left[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla^2 + v(\vec{r}) \right] \psi_i(\vec{r}) = \epsilon_i \psi_i(\vec{r}) $$ The wave function can be replaced as $$ \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + v(\vec{r})\right] \underbrace{\psi_i(\vec{r}) \alpha(\sigma)}_{\psi_i(\vec{r},\sigma)} = \epsilon_i \psi_i(\vec{r},\sigma) \]
    \[ \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + v(\vec{r})\right] \underbrace{\psi_j(\vec{r}) \beta(\sigma)}_{\psi_j(\vec{r},\sigma)} = \epsilon_j \psi_j(\vec{r},\sigma) \]

    Energy Levels of Two Electron Systems

    Here the total Hamiltonian is $$ \left[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla^2 + v(\vec{r}) - \frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla'^2 + v(\vec{r}') \right] \psi(\vec{r}, \sigma; \vec{r}', \sigma') = E \, \psi(\vec{r}, \sigma; \vec{r}', \sigma') $$ where we can separate (separable Hamiltonian) the total wave function into the ones above $$ \left[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla^2 + v(\vec{r}) - \frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla'^2 + v(\vec{r}') \right] \psi_i(\vec{r}, \sigma) \psi_j(\vec{r}', \sigma') = (\epsilon_i + \epsilon_j) \psi_i(\vec{r}, \sigma) \psi_j(\vec{r}', \sigma') $$ Or similarly we get $$ \left[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla^2 + v(\vec{r}) - \frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla'^2 + v(\vec{r}') \right] \psi_j(\vec{r}, \sigma) \psi_i(\vec{r}', \sigma') = (\epsilon_j + \epsilon_i) \psi_j(\vec{r}, \sigma) \psi_i(\vec{r}', \sigma') $$ Recall that for fermions, we need antisymmetric wave function, so we separate the variables into spatial and spin wave functions like below $$ \begin{aligned} \psi_i(\vec{r}, \sigma) &= \psi_i(\vec{r}) \chi_i(\sigma) \quad & \psi_j(\vec{r}', \sigma') &= \psi_j(\vec{r}') \chi_j(\sigma') \ \psi_j(\vec{r}, \sigma) &= \psi_j(\vec{r}) \chi_j(\sigma) \quad & \psi_i(\vec{r}', \sigma') &= \psi_i(\vec{r}') \chi_i(\sigma') \end{aligned} $$ and we arrive $$ \left[ \underbrace{-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla^2 + v(\vec{r})}{H(\vec{r}, \vec{p})} \underbrace{-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla'^2 + v(\vec{r}')} \right) $$ $$ = (\epsilon_i + \epsilon_j) \left( \frac{\psi_i(\vec{r}) \chi_i(\sigma) \psi_j(\vec{r}') \chi_j(\sigma') - \psi_j(\vec{r}) \chi_j(\sigma) \psi_i(\vec{r}') \chi_i(\sigma')}{\sqrt{2}} \right) $$}', \vec{p}')} \right] \left( \frac{\psi_i(\vec{r}) \chi_i(\sigma) \psi_j(\vec{r}') \chi_j(\sigma') - \psi_j(\vec{r}) \chi_j(\sigma) \psi_i(\vec{r}') \chi_i(\sigma')}{\sqrt{2}

    Here we can simplify this into shorter version i.e. Slater determinant $$ \psi(\vec{r}, \sigma; \vec{r}', \sigma') = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2!}} \begin{vmatrix} \psi_i(\vec{r}, \sigma) & \psi_i(\vec{r}', \sigma') \ \psi_j(\vec{r}, \sigma) & \psi_j(\vec{r}', \sigma') \end{vmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2!}} \begin{vmatrix} \psi_i(\vec{r})\chi_i(\sigma) & \psi_i(\vec{r}')\chi_i(\sigma') \ \psi_j(\vec{r})\chi_j(\sigma) & \psi_j(\vec{r}')\chi_j(\sigma') \end{vmatrix} $$

    Ground State

    Ground state occurs when the both electrons are in the ground state where the spins are opposite to each other: $$ \psi_{11}^{(s)}(\vec{r}, \sigma; \vec{r}', \sigma') = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2!}} \begin{vmatrix} \psi_1(\vec{r}) \alpha(\sigma) & \psi_1(\vec{r}') \alpha(\sigma') \ \psi_1(\vec{r}) \beta(\sigma) & \psi_1(\vec{r}') \beta(\sigma') \end{vmatrix} = \psi_1(\vec{r}) \psi_1(\vec{r}') \frac{1}{\sqrt{2!}} \begin{vmatrix} \alpha(\sigma) & \alpha(\sigma') \ \beta(\sigma) & \beta(\sigma') \end{vmatrix} $$

    Excited States

    There are four excited states: 1. down-down on ground and excited states 2. up-up on ground and excited states 3. up-down on ground and excited states 4. down-up on ground and excited states

    \[ \psi_{12}^{(t)}(\vec{r}, \sigma; \vec{r}', \sigma') = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{vmatrix} \psi_1(\vec{r})\alpha(\sigma) & \psi_1(\vec{r}')\alpha(\sigma') \\ \psi_2(\vec{r})\alpha(\sigma) & \psi_2(\vec{r}')\alpha(\sigma') \end{vmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{vmatrix} \psi_1(\vec{r}) & \psi_1(\vec{r}') \\ \psi_2(\vec{r}) & \psi_2(\vec{r}') \end{vmatrix} \underbrace{\alpha(\sigma)\alpha(\sigma')}_{|S=1, M=1\rangle} \]
    \[ \psi_{12}^{(t)'}(\vec{r}, \sigma; \vec{r}', \sigma') = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{vmatrix} \psi_1(\vec{r})\beta(\sigma) & \psi_1(\vec{r}')\beta(\sigma') \\ \psi_2(\vec{r})\beta(\sigma) & \psi_2(\vec{r}')\beta(\sigma') \end{vmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{vmatrix} \psi_1(\vec{r}) & \psi_1(\vec{r}') \\ \psi_2(\vec{r}) & \psi_2(\vec{r}') \end{vmatrix} \underbrace{\beta(\sigma)\beta(\sigma')}_{|S=1, M=-1\rangle} \]
    \[ \psi_{12}''(\vec{r}, \sigma; \vec{r}', \sigma') = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{vmatrix} \psi_1(\vec{r})\alpha(\sigma) & \psi_1(\vec{r}')\alpha(\sigma') \\ \psi_2(\vec{r})\beta(\sigma) & \psi_2(\vec{r}')\beta(\sigma') \end{vmatrix} \]
    \[ \psi_{12}'''(\vec{r}, \sigma; \vec{r}', \sigma') = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{vmatrix} \psi_1(\vec{r})\beta(\sigma) & \psi_1(\vec{r}')\beta(\sigma') \\ \psi_2(\vec{r})\alpha(\sigma) & \psi_2(\vec{r}')\alpha(\sigma') \end{vmatrix} \]

    Electron Repulsion

    So far we have not considered the repulsive force between electrons. We need first order perturbation to do so. The Hamiltonian is modified such that $$ H = \underbrace{h(\vec{r})}{H_0} + \underbrace{h(\vec{r}')}} + \underbrace{\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{1}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|}{V $$}

    By applying the first order perturbation: $$ E_{\text{triplet}} = \langle \psi_{12}^{(t)} | H_0 | \psi_{12}^{(t)} \rangle + \langle \psi_{12}^{(t)} | V_{ee} | \psi_{12}^{(t)} \rangle $$ $$ E_{\text{triplet}} = \epsilon_1 + \epsilon_2 + \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \int d^3r \int d^3r' \, \frac{|\psi_{12}^{(t)}(\vec{r}, \vec{r}')|^2}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|} $$

    Same goes to singlet.

    Symmetry on Repulsion energy

    For the triplet \(S=1\) states, note that the spatial wave function is antisymmetric, i.e. the electrons are avoiding each other, resulting in a lower repulsive interaction.

    For the singlet states, the spatial wave is symmetric, resulting in a higher repulsive interaction.

    Therefore the coulomb repulsive energies is lower for triplet states.
