    The liquid drop model does not explain the phenomena in excited state. Therefore we are going to use the atomic physics knowledge to explain, even though it is less precise and smaller range of applicability.

    There are some properties that can be explained using this model. For example, the magic numbers which require large amount of energy to transition and have larger binding energies

    Just like atomic physics where when we are considering the Zeeman shift, we calculate the energy shift due to the magnetic field. Here, we consider the energy levels modified by the infinite potential well, Saxon-Woods potential and the spin-orbit coupling.

    To do this, we have to make a few assumption: 1. The potential experienced by a nucleon is from other nucleons and it is spherically symmetry 2. The remaining \(A-1\) nucleons combine to have 0 total angular momentum (usually the case if \(A-1\) is even).

    The potential (Saxon-Woods) experienced can be approximate to roughly follow the nucleon density in Form Factor i.e. $\(V(r) = -\frac{V_0}{1+e^{{(r-r_0)}/a}}\)$ Solve the Schrodinger equation to get the influence of infinite potential well + Saxon Woods potential.

    What remain is the spin-orbit coupling, which is $$ H =V_{\text{LS}} = V_{LS}(r) \mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{S} $$

    Spin and parity of nuclei

    Recall that the parity of a state is given by its eigenvalue under the operation \(\mathbf{P} : \mathbf{x} \rightarrow -\mathbf{x}\)

    \[ \mathbf{P} Y_{L,m}(\theta, \phi) = Y_{L,m}(\pi - \theta, \phi + \pi) \rightarrow (-1)^L Y_{L,m}(\theta, \phi) \]

    The composite states have parity equal to the product of their individual parities. The shell model can predicy the parity and total angular momentum for ground states in certain cases:

    1. If the number of protons and neutrons are even, the expect the parity to be \(+1\) since for every nucleon we can find another with the same parity. The net angular momentum is zero because they pair up.
    2. If the number of protons and neutrons are odd-even, the parity and angular momentum are determined by the unpaired nucleon i.e. \(P = (-1)^L\)
    3. Odd-odd number: no prediction

    Excited states

    The transition between states can occur via emission or absorption of a photon. The essence for the nuclear selection rule lies in the conservation of parity and angular momentum.

    The photons can carry away non-zero angular momentum \(\ell \geq 1\) and it is related to the multipolarity of radiation i.e. - Dipole: \(\ell =1\) - Quadrupole: \(\ell = 2\) - Octupole: \(\ell = 3\)

    That means we have \(\mathbf{J}_i = \mathbf{J}_f + \vec{\ell}\) and that means the selection rule for angular momentum is $$ |J_i - J_f| \leq \ell \leq J_i + J_f $$ Note that if \(J_i = J_f =1\), we get \(\ell = 2\) it seems like there is no change in angular momentum but the photon carries non-zero momentum. This is because this is actually a vector equation, so even though it does not change in magnitude but it does change as vector.

    There are two ways to change the angular momentum: 1. Spin change 2. Orbital angular momentum change

    Spin change

    • Magnetic transition i.e. magnetic dipole/quadrupole/octapole
    • Labelled as \(M\ell\)
    • From matrix elements, the transition amplitude of magnetic transition is less than electric transition \(\implies\) less probable than electric transition.

    Orbital angular momentum change

    • Electric transition
    • Labelled as \(E\ell\)
    • More probable than electric transition

    The parity of the EM radiation is different electric and magnetic transition: $$ P_f = \begin{cases} P_i(-1)^\ell & \text{for } E\ell \text{ transitions} \ P_i(-1)^{\ell+1} & \text{for } M\ell \text{ transitions} \end{cases} $$ rearranging this gives $$ (-1)^\ell = \begin{cases} P_i P_f & \text{for an } E\ell \text{ transition} \ -P_i P_f & \text{for a } M\ell \text{ transition} \end{cases} $$

    It is evident that the parity conservation will allow transition \(E(\ell \pm (\text{even number}))\) and \(M(\ell \pm (\text{odd number}))\)
