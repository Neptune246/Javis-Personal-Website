    Spin

    Electron orbiting magnetic field

    When moving in a circular orbit, the area sweeped is \(A=\pi r^2\) and the current produced is \(I = -ev/(2\pi r)\) where \(v\) is the speed. The magnetic moment produced is then $\(\mu_l = IA = -\frac{e}{2m_e}L\)$

    In vector form $\(\vec{\mu_l} = -\frac{\mu_B}{\hbar}\vec{L}\)$ where the coefficient of \(L\) is replaced by Bohr magneton \(\mu_B = e\hbar / (2m_e)\)

    The operator is then \((\hat{\mu_l})_z = -\mu_B \hat{L_z} / \hbar = -\mu_B m_l \hbar / \hbar = -m_l \mu_B\). This shows that magnetic momentum in \(z\) direction is quantised. Remember that \(m_l\) is magnetic quantum number and is dependent on \(l\), orbital quantum number.

    Hydrogen atom in a magnetic field

    The magnetic potential energy is $$ V = -(\mu_l)_z B_z = m_l \mu_B B_z $$ and by differentiating it with respect to \(z\), gives $$ F_z = \left( \mu_l \right)_z \frac{\partial B_z}{\partial z} $$ where \(\left( \mu_l \right)_z = -m_l\mu_B\) as shown above. So based on this, then \(m_l = 0\) then there should be no effect from the magnetic field, however we can see from Stern-Gerlach experiment that the beam splitted into two.

    \(\implies\) there have to be another magnetic momentum which is not associated with orbital angular momentum and can 2 possible values rather than continuum of values (since it splitted into two).

    Spin magnetic momentum

    We slightly modify the magnetic momentum equation by replacing the orbital angular momentum \(\vec{L}\) by \(\vec{S}\) and add a factor called g-factor to it $$ \underline{\mu}_s = -g_s \frac{e}{2m_e} S = -g_s \frac{\mu_B}{\hbar} \vec{S} $$ \(\implies\) the \(z\) direction force is then $$ F_z = \left( \mu_s \right)_z \frac{\partial B_z}{\partial z} = -g_s m_s \mu_B \frac{\partial B_z}{\partial z} $$ Since the experimental result gives only two values, if we assume that \(m_s\) ranges from \(-s\) to \(+s\), like that of \(l\), then we must take \(s = 1/2\) and \(m_s = \pm 1/2\) because the steps must be an integer. Well, it simply means you can treat orbital angular momentum as spin. Everything is the same but different symbols.

    [!IMPORTANT] \(m_s = -s, -s+1, ... , s+1, s\)

    By working using \(m_s\) and spin magnetic moment, $\(S_z = \pm\frac{\hbar}{2}\)$

    Spin operators

    We define \(\vec{S}\) as the angular momentum spin operator. Similar to that of orbital angular momentum, the eigenvalues are

    [!important] \(s(s+1)\hbar^2\) for \(S^2\) \(m_s \hbar\) for \(S_z\)

    except that their eigenstates are not spherical harmonics and not defined by any angles \(\theta\) and \(\phi\).

    Instead for electrons, when it has a - spin up state \(s = 1/2 \implies m_s = +1/2 \implies\) eigenstate \(|\chi_+\rangle\)
    - spin down state \(s = 1/2 \implies m_s = -1/2 \implies\) eigenstate \(|\chi_-\rangle\)

    Formalism

    [!note] $\(S_z | \chi_+ \rangle = \left( +\frac{\hbar}{2} \right) | \chi_+ \rangle \quad\)$ $\(\quad S_z | \chi_- \rangle = \left( -\frac{\hbar}{2} \right) | \chi_- \rangle\)$

    We can merge them in a matrix and that matrix is called a Hermitian matrix $$ S_z = \frac{\hbar}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} $$

    We have also $$ \langle \chi_+ | \chi_+ \rangle = \langle \chi_- | \chi_- \rangle = 1 $$
