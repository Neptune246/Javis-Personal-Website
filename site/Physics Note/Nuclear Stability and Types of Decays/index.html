    Nuclear Stability and Types of Decays

    We characterise how long does a nucleus live by the frequency of decays or activity

    $$ A =\frac{dN}{dt} = \lambda N(t) \quad \Rightarrow \quad N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t} $$ Here we can work out the halflife of a sample, i.e. the time for a sample to decay to half of the number of atoms: $$ \begin{aligned} N(t_{1/2}) = \frac{N_0}{2} = N_0 e^{-\lambda t_{1/2}} \ \implies t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\lambda} \end{aligned} $$ where the mean lifetime (average time for a nucleus in the sample to decay) is given by \(\tau = t_{1/2}/\text{ln}2\)

    Beta Decays

    \(\beta^-\) decays

    This process occurs when the nucleus has more neutrons than protons. It converts neutrons to protons through $$ n \to p + e^- + \bar{\nu}e $$ and the resulting equation in terms of the proton and neutron number will be $$ ^A_ZX_N \rightarrow ^A_e $$} Y_{N-1} + e^- + \bar{\nu

    The initial energy is used to create an electron and 'make' the element \(Y\) to lack of an electron, there is no additional energy required for the neutron to be converted to proton i.e., the only requirement is that the energy of the mother nucleus has to be greater than the daughter nucleus: $$ M(A, Z) > M(A, Z+1) $$

    \(\beta^+\) decays

    This process occurs when the protons are more than the neutrons in the nucleus and the process is described by $$ p \to n + e^+ + \nu_e $$ where the equation of proton and neutron numbers is $$^A_Z X_N \rightarrow ^A_{Z-1}Y_{N+1} + e^+ + \nu_e $$ Note that the initial energy is used to create a positron and 'make' the daughter nucleus to have one excess electron, so additional energy is required for this process to be feasible: $\(M(A,Z) > M(A,Z-1) + 2m_e\)$

    Therefore, there is no free neutrons even though there are free protons (free neutrons do not need additional energy to decay).

    Electron Capture

    This process is only feasible when the wave function of the electron overlaps with the nucleus and it is typically \(s\)-shell electron because its radial wave function is non-zero at the centre of the atom.

    The process is $$ p + e^- \rightarrow n + \nu_e, $$ with $$ ^A_Z X_N + e^- \rightarrow ^A_{Z-1} Y_{N+1} + \nu_e $$ No energy is needed to produce a positron or electron, only negligible energy for the neutrino is needed: $$ M(A, Z) > M(A, Z - 1) + \epsilon $$ The binding energy of a nucleus is the difference between the atomic constituents and the atomic mass $$ B(A, Z) = \underbrace{Z M(^1\text{H}) + (A - Z) M(n)}_\text{mass of the atomic constituents} - M(A, Z) $$ where \(M(^1H) = M_p + m_e\) , \(M(n)\) is the mass of neutron and \(M(A,Z)\) is the mass of mother nucleus. Since \(A\) does not change we can combine this with the binding energy discussed in Nuclear Masses and Binding Energies to form $$ M(A, Z) = (A - Z)M_n + Z(M_p + m_e) - a_V A + a_s A^{2/3} + a_c \frac{Z^2}{A^{1/3}} + a_a \frac{(A - 2Z)^2}{4A} - \frac{\delta}{A^{1/2}} $$ For odd \(A\), we have only one parabola. Only one isobar is the lowest and is \(\beta\)-stable. Akk other isobars can decay to that stable isobar

    For even A, we have two parabolas (because of pairing term i.e. $$ \delta = \begin{cases} +\delta_p & \text{even-even nucleus} \ 0 & \text{odd-even nucleus} \ -\delta_p & \text{odd-odd nucleus} \end{cases} $$

    \(A\) changing decays

    As discussed above, the \(\beta\) decay does not change \(A\) but they can occur in 3 processes.

    1. Proton and neutron emission: Proton-, or neutron-rich nuclei emit a single proton or neutron first before decaying.
    \[ \text{Neuton emission:} \quad^{A}_{Z} X_N \rightarrow ^{A-1}_{Z} Y_{N-1} + n $$ $$ \text{Proton emission:} \quad ^{A}_{Z} X_N \rightarrow ^{A-1}_{Z-1} Y_N + p \]

    where $$ \text{Neutron emission:} \quad M(A, Z) > M(A - 1, Z) + M_n $$

    \[ \text{Proton emission:} \quad M(A, Z) > M(A - 1, Z - 1) + M(^1\text{H}) \]
    1. \(\alpha\)-decay: This typically happens in a heavy nucleus.
    \[ ^{A}_{Z} X_N \rightarrow ^{A-4}_{Z-2} Y_{N-2} + ^{4}_{2} \text{He}_2 \]

    where energy has to fulfil $$ M(A, Z) > M(A - 4, Z - 2) + M(4, 2) $$ We can predict the probability of the nucleus of emitting an \(\alpha\) particle. We first construct the potential wall which consists of coulomb interaction outside of the atomic radius and strong force inside ![[Pasted image 20260417090002.png]]

    By solving the Schrodinger equation, we get the probability of the particle tunnels out through the thin wall $$ \Psi(x) = \exp \left( -\int_{0}^{x} dx \sqrt{2m(V(x) - E)} \right) \equiv e^{-G} $$ where \(G\) is the Gamow factor.
