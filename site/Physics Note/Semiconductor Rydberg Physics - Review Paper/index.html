    Semiconductor Rydberg Physics Review Paper

    The binding energies of electronic states can be written in terms of principle quantum number. $\(E_n = -\frac{E_{Ryd}}{(n-\delta)^2}\)$

    Rydberg atoms can be approximated to hydrogen-like systems, with usual Coulomb interaction energy. The Rydberg energy (unit of energy to ionise a hydrogen atom) is $\(E_{Ryd} = \frac{\mu e^4}{32\epsilon_0^2 \epsilon_r^2 \pi^2 \hbar^2}\)$ where \(\mu\) is a reduced mass of two charges.

    General differences between excitons in semiconductors and Rydberg atoms

    Feature Excitons in semiconductors Rydberg atoms
    Relative Permittivity \(\epsilon_r\) Greater than one (excitons in a medium that screens Coulomb interactions) 0 as they are treated as hydrogen-like systems
    Masses of Electrons and Protons Effective masses of electrons and protons are magnitude-alike but will be lower than free electron mass Usual electron's and proton's masses (and different magnitude)

    Implication of the differences

    • Both effects (excitons) lower the Rydberg energy, and hence the binding energy
    • Reduction in Rydberg energy due to effective masses depends on materials, at least by a factor of 2
    • The dielectric constant of Cuprite scales with $\(\kappa \propto n^4\)$ where \(n\) is the refractive index (\(n\) = 2.7 for Cuprite)

    The paper quantifies the reduction in Rydberg energy compared to hydrogen (on page 3).

    Differences between cold atoms and excitons

    Feature Cold atoms Excitons
    Surroundings Individual entity that may be placed in vacuum Exist in semiconductor environment and impossible to decouple them from surroundings (defects like charged impurities and phonons).
    Open systems 1S state is the true ground state (is not open system) Necessarily constitute open systems (coupled to an environment)
    Confinement Easy to be confined (trapping Rydberg atoms in space with high precision) Randomly created in the light beam, harder to control the positions.

    Note: Open systems are (a subsystem that is coupled to a larger, complex environment which defines its very existence and properties.)

    Note: It's difficult to study a truly isolated state because the energy levels are close and phonons may cause them to transition

    What renders a material suitable to host Rydberg excitons

    • High exciton binding energy (but binding energy scales as \(n^{-4}\), and max principle quantum number is \(n_{max} = \sqrt{E_B/k_BT}\) Materials with small binding energy have considerable amount of inhomogeneous broadening)
    • Peculiar band structure (same parity, no transition between states, giving high binding energy. Only P states can have direct dipole transition - that means no dipole emission at \(n =1\))

    Rydberg exciton blockade

    • Energy required to create a second exciton in the vicinity of the first one becomes modified by their interaction energy
