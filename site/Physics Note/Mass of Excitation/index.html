    Mass of Excitation

    Amplitude mode (Non-continuous symmetry breaking)

    Recall that the Ginzburg-Landau model is $\(f = f_0 + \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{\nabla}\phi)^2}_{\text{"Kinetic energy" } K} \pm \underbrace{\frac{\mu^2}{2}\phi^2 + \frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^4}_{\text{"Potential energy" } U}\)$ For amplitude modes when \(T>T_c\) the potential is $\(U(\phi) = \frac{\mu^2}{2} \phi^2 + \frac{\lambda}{4!} \phi^4 \approx \frac{\mu^2}{2} \phi^2\)$ where \(\mu = m\). For \(T<T_c\) we have $\(U(\phi) = \frac{\mu^2}{2} \phi^2 + \frac{\lambda}{4!} \phi^4\)$ Now we want to prove that this excitation requires energy. We first find the minimum point: $\(\left. \frac{\partial U}{\partial \phi} \right|_{\phi_0} = -\mu^2 \phi_0 + \frac{\lambda}{3!} \phi_0^3 = 0 \implies \phi_0 = \left( \frac{6\mu^2}{\lambda} \right)^{1/2}\)$ and expand around it $\(\begin{aligned} U(\phi) &\approx U(\phi_0) + \frac{1}{2} \left. \left( \frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial \phi^2} \right) \right|_{\phi_0} (\phi - \phi_0)^2 + \cdots \\[1em] \left. \left( \frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial \phi^2} \right) \right|_{\phi_0} &= -\mu^2 + \frac{\lambda}{2}\phi_0^2 = -\mu^2 + \frac{\lambda}{2}\left( \frac{6\mu^2}{\lambda} \right) = 2\mu^2 \\[1em] \implies U(\phi) &\approx U(\phi_0) + \mu^2 (\phi - \phi_0)^2 + \cdots \end{aligned}\)$

    By comparing this to the case where \(T>T_c\) i.e. it turns from \(\mu^2/2\) to \(\mu^2\): $\(\frac{m^2}{2} = \mu \implies m = \sqrt{2}\mu\)$ i.e. the excitation causes increase in mass by a factor of \(\sqrt{2}\).

    Goldstone mode (continuous symmetry breaking)

    Goldstone mode is a different story as it is the rolling the gutter mode. We therefore have to look at two directions: \(\phi_1\) and \(\phi_2\)

    Note that this has a rotational symmetry i.e. we can obtain our free energy from there. At high temperature, $\(\begin{aligned} &\begin{pmatrix} \phi'_1 \\ \phi'_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \phi_1 \\ \phi_2 \end{pmatrix} \\[1em] &f(\phi'_1, \phi'_2) = f(\phi_1, \phi_2) \qquad \qquad \text{['SO(2)' symmetry]} \\[1em] &\boxed{f = \frac{1}{2}\left[ (\mathbf{\nabla}\phi_1)^2 + (\mathbf{\nabla}\phi_2)^2 \right] \pm \frac{\mu^2}{2}(\phi_1^2 + \phi_2^2) + \frac{\lambda}{4!}(\phi_1^2 + \phi_2^2)^2} \end{aligned}\)$

    At low temperature, we have $\(U = -\frac{\mu^2}{2}(\phi_1^2 + \phi_2^2) + \frac{\lambda}{4!}(\phi_1^2 + \phi_2^2)^2\)$ By letting \(x^2 = \phi_1^2 + \phi_2^2\), we express the global minimum in terms of \(x\): $\(\left. \frac{\partial U}{\partial x} \right|_{x_0} = -\mu^2 x_0 + \frac{\lambda}{3!} x_0^3 = 0\)$Here we choose a minimum to simplify the calculation: $\(\phi_1 = \left(\frac{6\mu^2}{\lambda}\right)^{1/2}, \qquad \phi_2 = 0\)$

    Again, we expand around the chosen minimum point $\(\begin{aligned} &U(\phi) \approx U(\phi_0) + \frac{1}{2} \left. \left( \frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial \phi^2} \right) \right|_{\phi_0} (\phi - \phi_0)^2 + \cdots = \\[1em] &\implies \frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial \phi_1^2} = -\mu^2 + \frac{\lambda}{3!} (\phi_1^2 + \phi_2^2) + \frac{2\lambda}{3!} \phi_1^2 = -\mu^2 + \frac{\lambda}{2} \phi_1^2 + \frac{\lambda}{3!} \phi_2^2 \\[1em] & \qquad\implies \frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial \phi_1^2} = -\mu^2 + \frac{\lambda}{2} \frac{6\mu^2}{\lambda} = 2\mu^2 \qquad \text{Evaluate at the minimum, } \left[ \left(\frac{6\mu^2}{\lambda}\right)^{1/2}, 0 \right] \\[1em] &\implies \frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial \phi_2^2} = -\mu^2 + \frac{\lambda}{2}\phi_2^2 + \frac{\lambda}{3!}\phi_1^2\\[1em] & \qquad \implies \frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial \phi_2^2} = -\mu^2 + \frac{\lambda}{3!}\frac{6\mu^2}{\lambda} = 0 \qquad \text{Evaluate at the minimum, } \left[ \left(\frac{6\mu^2}{\lambda}\right)^{1/2}, 0 \right] \end{aligned}\)$ By comparing it to high temperature where $\(U = -\frac{\mu^2}{2}(\phi_1^2 + \phi_2^2) + \frac{\lambda}{4!}(\phi_1^2 + \phi_2^2)^2\)$ we realise that the \(\phi_1\) mode (amplitude mode) has an increase of mass i.e. $\(\frac{m^2}{2} = \mu^2\)$ consistent with the results we had in amplitude mode. And the \(\phi^2\) excitation have zero mass

    This is the result of Goldstone's theorem i.e. breaking a continuous symmetry leads to massless excitations.
