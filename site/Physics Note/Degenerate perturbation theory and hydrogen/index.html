    Degenerate perturbation theory and hydrogen

    As we treat hydrogen based on the Schrodinger equation, we have ignored relativistic effect. It is not negligible and should be treated as perturbations.

    Degeneracy in hydrogen

    Energy level \(n\) in hydrogen is - \(n^2\) degenerate (without spin) - 2\(n^2\) degenerate (with spin, with additional \(m_s = \pm 1/2\))

    Spin-orbit coupling (a perturbation to energy of a particle of non-heavy elements)

    Idea

    Classically, we say the electron is orbiting the nucleus in nucleus and lab view. However, due to relativistic effect, in the electron's view, the nucleus is orbiting around the electron and hence, it experiences an electric and magnetic fields (due to moving charge particles). The magnetic field couples \(L\) and \(s\) which will perturb the energy of electrons and cause an energy level splitting.

    Nuclei do not experience magnetic field like electrons do even though it sees electrons orbiting around them. This is because in lab view, nuclei are stationary and have zero spin. So they experience (if any) negligible effect of SOC

    • Heavy atoms like gold, bismuth and lead do not experience SOC as a mere perturbation. Instead, because their inner electrons move very close to the speed of light, so we should include the term in our initial Hamiltonian.

    We ultimately want to find the perturbed potential due to the magnetic dipole momentum generated by electron spin i.e. $$ H'_{\text{so}} = -\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_s \cdot \underline{\boldsymbol{B}} $$

    Finding \(\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_s\)

    Recall that the spin angular momentum is (info here) \(\mu_s = -g_s \left( \frac{e}{2m_e} \right) S\) where the g-factor is approximately 2. So the equation is $$ \underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_s = -\frac{e}{m_e} \boldsymbol{S} $$

    Finding \(\underline{\boldsymbol{B}}\)

    $$ L = r m_e v = \frac{r m_e 2\pi r}{P} = \frac{2\pi m_e r^2}{P} = \frac{2\pi m_e r^2 I}{e} = \frac{4\pi m_e r^3 B}{\mu_0 e} $$ where \(B = \mu_0 I/2r\) and \(I = e/P\) where \(P = 2\pi r/v\) is the orbital period

    Combining

    It gives $$ H_{\text{so}}' = -\boldsymbol{\mu}_s \cdot \boldsymbol{B} = \frac{e}{m} \boldsymbol{S} \cdot \left( \frac{e}{4\pi\epsilon_0 c^2 m r^3} \boldsymbol{L} \right) = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 c^2 m^2 r^3} \boldsymbol{S} \cdot \boldsymbol{L} $$

    But due to special relativity, the correct perturbation term is being approximately half in magnitude:

    $\(H_{\text{so}}' = \frac{e^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0 c^2 m^2 r^3} \boldsymbol{S} \cdot \boldsymbol{L}\)$and it matches with the experimental findings. This effect is called Thomas' precession and takes into account relativistic time dilation between the electron and the nucleus.

    Commutation properties

    Remember the smart operator we mentioned in Degenerate perturbation theory II? We are going to use the total angular momentum \(\boldsymbol{J} = \boldsymbol{L} + \boldsymbol{S}\) where \(J^2\) and \(J_z\) commute with all of our original operators and with the perturbation

    \[ [J^2, \boldsymbol{L} \cdot \boldsymbol{S}] = 0 $$ $$ [J^2, \boldsymbol{S} \cdot \boldsymbol{L}] = 0 \]

    So we are going to use joint eigenfunctions of operators \(H^0, \quad L^2, \quad J^2, \quad S^2, \quad J_z\)

    Maximum and minimum values of \(m_J\)

    Recall that - \(m_l = -l, -l+1, ..., 0, ..., l-1, l\) - \(m_s = -s, ..., s\) - \(s = 1/2\) for electron

    Hence the maximum value of \(m_J\) is \(m_J = l + s\) and minimum value is \(m_J = -l-s\). The range runs in integer steps, the eigenvalue is \(j(j+1)\hbar^2\)

    Basically: - Eigenvalue of \(L^2\) is \(l(l+1)\) - Eigenvalue of \(S^2\) is \(s(s+1)\) - Eigenvalue of \(J^2\) is \(j(j+1)\)

    The range of \(j\) is \(|l-s| < j < l+s\) and \(-j < m_j < j\)

    Fun fact

    Gold’s yellow hue arises partly because SOC lowers the energy of its 5\(d\) electrons, shifting absorption from UV to visible blue. This reflects complementary yellow light!
