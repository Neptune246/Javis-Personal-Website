    Introduction and Bonding

    This is an extension of Bonding.

    Metallic Bonding

    • Periodic array of atomic cores within a sea of delocalised electrons which are described by nearly-free electron model
    • In reciprocal space, the electrons fill states within the Fermi surface wwith energy \(E = \hbar^2 k_F^2/2m\)

    The potential is described by $\(U(r) = -\frac{a}{r} + \frac{b}{r^2}\)$

    Covalent Bonding

    The electron density for pure covalent bond is symmetrical. When the electron density cloud for two atoms overlap, it forms a region with less/more electron density depending on the type of interference.

    For destructive interference, the centre region has less electron density and hence forming antibonding molecular orbital

    Otherwise, the centre region has more electron density and form a bonding molecular orbital

    The quantum mechanical derivation is outlined in Bonding. The extend of interaction and stabilisation of the bonding combination depends on the energy difference between the antibonding and bonding energy levels. For example

    HF has a larger energy difference between the antibonding \(3\sigma^*\) and \(2\sigma\) and the former is closer to the 1s atomic orbital while the latter is closer to the 2p atomic orbital. This means that there is less mixing between states and more of ionic property and it will be discussed below.

    In contrast, HCl has less energy difference and more mixing between states.

    These molecular orbital will overlap each other in solids and form a band where the bonding levels will overlap to form valence band and antibonding forms conduction band.

    Since the band gaps and the widths of the conduction band depend on the interatomic distance, it allows us to find which elements are metals or insulator or semiconductor

    Ionic Bonding

    When the relative energy between the interacting atoms becomes large, the covalent bonding is effectively turning into ionic bonding.

    Since all interactions are attractive in close range and repulsive in long range, ionic bonding is attractive in close range because of Coulomb force and repulsive because of the electronic repulsion.

    Therefore we are looking for the equilibrium between these two forces i.e. the overall lattice energy is $\(U_{\text{lattice}} = U_{\text{Mad}} + U_{\text{rep}} = \alpha \frac{N_A Q_+ Q_-}{4\pi \epsilon_0 r} + m N_A B e^{\frac{-r}{\rho}}\)$ where \(U_\text{Mad}\) is Madelung attractive energy and \(U_\text{rep}\) is the Born-Mayer repulsive energy. \(N_A\) is the Avogadro's constant, \(B\) and \(\rho\) are the constants to be determined.

    Electronegativity

    To classify the bonding of two elements, it is easier to define a new concept: electronegativity which means the ability of an atom to attract draw electron cloud towards itself.

    Bonding between atoms with very different electronegativities will be predominantly ionic.

    Intermolecular Bonding

    Hydrogen Bonding

    • Interaction between an positive dipole \(\delta^+\) and the lone pair of electrons in very electronegative elements such as FON (Fluorine, Oxygen and Nitrogen).

    van Der Waals

    This is common in soft matter physics.

    • Temporary dipole-dipole interaction
    • Fluctuating electron density induces transient dipole

    This dipole from species 1 has an electric field of $\(E=\frac{p_1}{2\pi\epsilon_0r^3}\)$ Depending on the polarisability of species 2 which described by \(p_2 = \alpha E\) the potential energy is then $\(U = \frac{-|p_1| |p_2|}{4\pi \epsilon_0 r^3} = \frac{-|p_1| \chi E}{4\pi \epsilon_0 r^3} = \frac{-|p_1|^2 \chi}{(4\pi \epsilon_0 r^3)^2}\)$ Thus the force of this is \(f\propto r^{-7}\). This is then balanced with the repulsive force described by Lennard-Jones potential which arised from the cohesive energy i.e. energy needed to separate constituent pairs of species. $\(U_{LJ Cohesive} = -2E_0 \left[ A_6 \left( \frac{\sigma}{r_{ij}} \right)^6 - A_{12} \left( \frac{\sigma}{r_{ij}} \right)^{12} \right]\)$
