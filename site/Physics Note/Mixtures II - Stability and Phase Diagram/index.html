    Mixtures II Stability and Phase Diagram

    As a summary of the last note: We have initially a straight line if we dont consider the interfacial energy and the solution is not mixed. We then have a curve with two minima and a maximum when \(\phi = 1/2\) if we consider the interfacial energy.

    Demixing under the Regular Solution model

    There are two variable that oppose each other

    1. \(\kappa\)
    2. \(T\)

    The goal of this chapter is to find the mixture compositions at \(\phi_{\text{min1}}\) and \(\phi_{\text{min2}}\)

    At \(\phi_{\text{min1}}\) and \(\phi_{\text{min2}}\) , there must be specific mixture compositions that are particularly stable. We expect the mixture to decompose from \(\phi^*\) to \(\phi_{\text{min1}}\) and \(\phi_{\text{min2}}\) $$ \phi^ = \alpha \, \phi_{min1} + (1 - \alpha) \, \phi_{min2}, \quad 0 < \alpha < 1 $$ From there, we can calculate the decreases of free energy upon demixing $$ \bar{G}_{demix}^ = \alpha \, \bar{G}_1 + (1 - \alpha) \, \bar{G}_2 $$ and it can be shown that \(\bar{G}_{demix}^*\) is lower than the free energy of the unmixed system at the same composition. That's why there are dips (decrease from the pure A or pure B). Hint in the lecture note. From the graph, it also shows that the demixing in pure A and B is more energetically favourable than the mixing at \(\phi = 1/2\)

    Just remember - Free energy higher than the straight line is mixing, - Below than is mixing

    Mode of demixing and composition stability

    More complex stuff can happen and using free energy to determine the mixing/demixing processes of liquids is not enough. Sometimes it is possible for a system to exhibit \(\Delta \bar{G}^*_{mix} < 0\) but still demix.

    Demixing from a concave (with respect to the x-axis) region

    By looking at the region \(\phi^* \pm d\phi\) and analyse the trend, we can see if the free energy is locally convex, it can undergo spontaneous demix due to small fluctuation (note the position of the straight line in \(\phi^* \pm d\phi\)) as the free energy is decreasing locally.

    This spontaneous demixing process is called a spinodal decomposition

    Demixing from a convex (with respect to the x-axis) region

    By looking at the region \(\phi^* \pm d\phi\) and analyse the trend, we can see if the free energy is locally concave, it cannot undergo spontaneous demix (note the position of the straight line in \(\phi^* \pm d\phi\)) as the free energy is increasing locally.

    When it eventually takes place, the resulting spontaneous demixing process is called nucleation.

    Formalism

    If we are looking for a global favourable, we can just use the free energy. If we are looking for the stability (how the change in energy occurs with perturbation) of each state, we need second derivative. Because there exist a metastable state that is stable but not global minimum.

    The second derivative test comes from the Taylor expansion where \(\phi \rightarrow \phi+d\phi\) and \(\Delta f = \frac{1}{2} \frac{d^2 f}{d\phi^2}(\delta \phi)^2\)

    Unstable system have a decrease in energy $$ \frac{\partial^2 \Delta \bar{G}{mix}}{\partial \phi^2} = \frac{\partial^2 \bar{G} < 0 $$ For metastable (convex) $$ \frac{\partial^2 \Delta \bar{G}}}{\partial \phi^2{mix}}{\partial \phi^2} = \frac{\partial^2 \bar{G} > 0 $$ The inflexion point $}}{\partial \phi^2\(\frac{\partial^2 \Delta \bar{G}_{mix}}{\partial \phi^2} = \frac{\partial^2 \bar{G}_{mix}}{\partial \phi^2} = 0\)$ is called the spinodal
