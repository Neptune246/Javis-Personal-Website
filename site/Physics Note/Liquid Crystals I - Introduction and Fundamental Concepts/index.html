    Liquid Crystals I Introduction and Fundamental Concepts

    • Liquid Crystal is a state of matter which is an intermediate between a liquid and crystal.
    • It has some form of order and possesses fluidity
    • E.g. colloidal suspensions, polymers, amphiphilic systems
    • LC forming materials are called mesogens

    Liquid crystal phases

    1. Nematic (common): Orientational order but no positional order
    2. Smectic: Orientational and 1D positional order
    3. Columnar: Orientational order and 2D positional order
    4. Cholesterics: formed by chiral molecules.

    The initial state is called isotropic and it can phase transition to ordered LC phases by decreasing temperature (thermotropic) or increasing molecular density (lyotropic)

    Terminology

    To understand LC, we have to understand some technical terms:

    Director

    • Unit vector \(\vec{n}\) pointing to the average molecular direction

    • To describe the group alignment of the LC, we introduce the orientation function \(f(\theta)\); \(f(\theta)d\Omega\) is the fraction of molecules in a solid angle \(d\Omega\) that are oriented \(\theta\) from the director. Therefore, for a more ordered LC, the LCs will mostly distributed at \(\theta = 0, \pi\)

    Order parameter

    See Broken Symmetry as well.

    • Order parameter is a parameter that changes its value during phase transition.
    • Here, we defined it, \(\overline{\overline{Q}}\) as average alignment of the molecules to the director.
    • This is a tensor order parameter

    For the case of uniaxial (molecular distribution function for the LC molecules has an axial symmetry) nematic LC (which is the focus in this course), the order parameter is $$ Q_{\alpha \beta} = \left\langle u_{\alpha} u_{\beta} - \frac{1}{3} \delta_{\alpha \beta} \right\rangle = Q \left( n_{\alpha} n_{\beta} - \frac{1}{3} \delta_{\alpha \beta} \right) $$ where \(\vec{u}\) is the unit vector, \(Q\) is the largest eigenvalue/scalar order parameter of \(\overline{\overline{Q}}\) and \(\vec{n}\) is the eigenvector of \(\overline{\overline{Q}}\) with the largest eigenvalue. Intuitively, the largest eigenvalue is the most probable/aligned LC to the director.

    For a nematic with an angle distribution \(f(\theta)\) between \(\langle \vec{r} \rangle\) and \(\vec{n}\), the scalar order parameter is then $$ Q = \frac{3}{2} \left\langle (\vec{u} \cdot \vec{n})^2 - \frac{1}{3} \right\rangle = \frac{3}{2} \langle \cos^2 \theta - \frac{1}{3} \rangle = \int_{\Omega} \frac{3}{2} \left( \cos^2 \theta - \frac{1}{3} \right) f(\theta) \, d\Omega $$

    [!note] Note that the expectation value is $\(\langle x\rangle = \int xf(\theta)d\theta\)$ for a real-valued \(f(\theta)\)

    Nematic-Isotropic transition

    Here we use Helmholtz free energy instead of Gibbs free energy, since pressure is not necessarily conserved but the volume is conserved $\(F=U-TS\)$

    When transition from nematic to isotropic, since the LC is becoming more orientated, the entropy decreases and it causes an increase in the Helmholtz free energy. This is unfavourable. There are two possible factors:

    1. Maier-Saupe theory: LC molecules have attractive interaction and they have lower internal energy when the molecules are aligned.

    2. Onsager theory: When the LC molecules are aligned, there are more ways to arrange these rod-like molecules, which gives a higher entropy.

    Orientational entropy

    To derive an expression for Helmholtz free energy, we need to calculate the orientational entropy. We assume:

    • uniaxial nematic: only depends on the polar angle \(\theta\)
    • \(f(\theta)\) is normalised: \(\int f(\theta) d\Omega =1\)

    Using Boltzmann's formula, we obtain i.e. $\(S_{\text{orient}} = - k_B\sum P_i \text{ln}P_i = - k_B \int_\Omega f(\theta) \ \text{ln} \ (f(\theta))\)$ where we have a continuous distribution of \(\theta\) (turning summation into integration).
