    Capillary forces

    • Ability of a liquid to flow in very narrow spaces without external forces
    • Originates from surface tension of liquids that are in contact with solids
    • Liquid molecules have a stronger bonds than gas so they pull the interfacial liquid molecules away from the interface
    • If the liquid surface is curved then there is a pressure inside the liquid. At equilibrium, there is a pressure difference across the interface.

    For a more complicated case like a capillary bridge,

    the pressure difference is dependent on the curvature. $$ \Delta P \equiv P_{in} - P_{out} = \gamma_L C = \gamma_L \left( \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} \right) $$

    where \(\gamma_L\) is the surface tension of the liquid and \(P_{\text{in}}\) is the Laplace pressure. If the liquid structures are large enough, then the influence of gravity (hydrostatic pressure) has to be considered: $$ \Delta P = \gamma_L \left( \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} \right) + \rho gh $$

    The threshold for considering the hydrostatic pressure is $$ x_c = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma_L}{\rho g}} $$ where \(x_c\) is the balance of surface tension against gravitational force. For structures much smaller than \(x_c\), influence of gravity can be neglected.

    Capillary rise

    When a capillary is inserted into a liquid, the level of liquid in the tube is seen to go up or down by a height \(h\) with respect to the surface of the liquid.

    We want to determine the change of \(h\). To do so, we have to consider when the free energy is minimum i.e. \(dG/dh = 0\)

    The free energy is $$ dG = 2\pi r_c \, dh \, (\gamma_{SL} - \gamma_{SG}) + \pi r_c^2 \rho gh \, dh $$ $$\implies h = \frac{2(\gamma_{SG} - \gamma_{SL})}{r_c \rho g} = \frac{2\gamma_{LG} \cos \theta_{SL}}{r_c \rho g} $$

    Particles at the surface of a liquid

    1. Small particles

      • Defined as particles which are not significantly influenced by gravity and buoyancy
      • If the contact angle is zero (fully wetting) then the particle sinks into the liquid
      • If the contact is larger than zero then it floats on the interface without miniscus.

    2. Large particles

      • Gravity and buoyancy has the ability to move the particle
      • Liquid deformed by the contact angle of the particle

    To evaluate how stable the large particle is at the interface, we want to calculate the Gibbs free energy change before and after the particle is pushed into the liquid.

    $$ \Delta G = 2\pi R^2 (1 - \cos \theta)(\gamma_{SL} - \gamma_{SG}) + \pi R^2 \sin^2 \theta \, \gamma_{SG} = \pi R^2 \gamma_{LG} (\cos \theta - 1)^2 $$ For \(\Delta G >0\), the particle sinks

    Homogeneous nucleation

    • Supercooling of water.

    Without any disturbances and impurities as the the nucleus, the freezing process involves two types of Gibbs free energy at equilibrium

    Solid-liquid interface

    As mentioned in Surfaces and Interfaces 1 that the interfaces increase the energy in the system.

    As the water begins to freeze, an interface is formed between solid and liquid.

    This has a Gibbs free energy of $$ \Delta G_{\text{interf.}} = 4\pi r^2 \gamma_{\text{{SL}}}> 0 $$

    Volume free energy change

    As the chemical potential (Gibbs free energy per particle) of the molecules in solid state, \(\mu_s\) is lower than in liquid state \(\mu_L\), there is a decrease of free energy proportional to the volume of the nucleus.

    $$ \Delta G_{\text{phase}} = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \rho (\mu_s - \mu_L) < 0 $$ And the total free energy change of the system due to nucleation is

    $$ \Delta G(r) = \Delta G_{\text{interf.}}(r) + \Delta G_{\text{phase}}(r) $$

    There is an energy barrier opposing the transition, meaning that there is an unstable equilibrium between two transitions.

    However, if there is external perturbation, it promotes growth of the nucleus and lowers the free energy of the system, leading to freezing. Or if there is a nucleus site, i.e. impurities, it lowers the interfacial energy (because in this case, the nucleation occurs around the impurity instead of costing energy to make a solid-liquid interface) which allows heterogeneous nucleation and it is why the ice forms from the edges of the pond.
