    Bonding

    [!note] # Types of bonding 1. Metallic - Electron gas (freely-moving electrons in the positive metal ion) - Weak, which results in low melting temperature 2. Ionic - Electrostatic force - Central force that follows the inverse square law (\(-q^2/r^2\)) - The potential energy is $\(V = -\frac{A}{r^m} + \frac{B}{r^n}\)$ where \(n>m\) 3. Covalent - Quantum interaction - Force varies exponentially i.e. \(e^{-x/\lambda}\) - Directional with high melting point 4. Van Der Waal - Force is proportional to \(-1/r^7\) - The force is repulsive when two atoms or molecules are close together and becomes attractive after their equilibrium point.

    [!Understanding] Covalent case is complex. It exhibits quantum mechanical system which can be explained using the Principle of superposition and the bonding and antibonding molecular orbitals.

    • Bonding orbitals form when the wavefunctions of the 1s atomic orbital interfere constructively with each other
    • Antibonding orbitals form when there's destructive interference of wavefunctions. For hydrogen atoms, the two electrons (one from each atom) fills the bonding orbital, resulting in a lower overall energy system than when they are in their isolated state.

    For Helium atoms, initially the two electrons in each 1s orbital have already occupy the bonding orbital, hence the other two electrons must occupy the antibonding orbital. The energy reduction is cancelled by the energy increase from the antibonding electrons, hence the system is not stable.

    [!Derivation of bonding and antibonding orbital] Let us take the trial wavefunction of two electrons $\(|\psi\rangle = \phi_1 |1\rangle + \phi_2 |2\rangle\)$ where \(\phi_1\) and \(\phi_2\) are complex coefficient, \(|1\rangle\) and \(|2\rangle\) are the ground state orbital of an electron bound to the first nucleus and second nucleus respectively.

    We can observe that the relationship between \(|1\rangle\) , \(|2\rangle\) and the energies: $\((K + V_1) |1\rangle = \epsilon_0 |1\rangle\)$ and $\((K + V_2) |2\rangle = \epsilon_0 |2\rangle\)$ respectively where \(K + V_i\) is the Hamiltonian, \(H\) of the system.

    We assume that \(|1\rangle\) and \(|2\rangle\) (not correct when the electrons are very close) are orthogonal to each other such that their inner product is 0. This allows us to choose normalisation.

    An effective Schrodinger equation has the form of $\(\sum_{j} H_{ij} \phi_{j} = E \phi_{i}\)$ where \(H_{ij} = \langle i | H | j \rangle\). From there, we work out the following $\(H_{11} = \langle 1 | H | 1 \rangle = \langle 1 | K + V_{1} | 1 \rangle + \langle 1 | V_{2} | 1 \rangle = \epsilon_{0} + V_{cross}\)$ $\(H_{22} = \langle 2 | H | 2 \rangle = \langle 2 | K + V_{2} | 2 \rangle + \langle 2 | V_{1} | 2 \rangle = \epsilon_{0} + V_{cross}\)$ $\(H_{12} = \langle 1 | H | 2 \rangle = \langle 1 | K + V_{2} | 2 \rangle + \langle 1 | V_{1} | 2 \rangle = 0 - t\)$ $\(H_{21} = \langle 2 | H | 1 \rangle = \langle 2 | K + V_{2} | 1 \rangle + \langle 2 | V_{1} | 1 \rangle = 0 - t^{*}.\)$ where \(V_{cross}\) is direct term and \(t\) is exchange/hopping term. If we turn this into a matrix: $\(\left(\begin{array}{cc} \epsilon_{0} + V_{cross} & -t \\ -t^{*} & \epsilon_{0} + V_{cross} \end{array}\right) \left(\begin{array}{c} \phi_{1} \\ \phi_{2} \end{array}\right) = E \left(\begin{array}{c} \phi_{1} \\ \phi_{2} \end{array}\right).\)$ and diagonalise it, we get $\(E_{\pm} = \epsilon_0 + V_{cross} \pm |t|\)$ where the one with higher energy is the bonding orbital and the lower is the antibonding orbital. Their corresponding wavefunction is $\(\begin{array}{rcl} \left|\psi_{bonding}\right\rangle &=& \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left( \left|1\right\rangle \pm \left|2\right\rangle \right) \\ \left|\psi_{antibonding}\right\rangle &=& \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left( \left|1\right\rangle \mp \left|2\right\rangle \right). \end{array}\)$
